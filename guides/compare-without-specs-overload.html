    How to compare options without getting lost in specs

    Focus on the few criteria that change your decision and set the rest aside.

    Guide · 5 min read

    A specification table is an argument you’ve lost

    Given a spreadsheet of 30 specs, people stall, then pick the most popular row. That’s not deciding — it’s giving up. The fix is to shrink the table before you read it.

    Most specs are irrelevant to your decision. The only ones that matter are the ones on which your shortlist of requirements would behave differently. Everything else is noise that makes the signal harder to see.

    Build your table around three columns

    Keep one column for each thing that matters, and a final one for the trade-off. A usable comparison rarely needs more than this:

    1. Fits your use case? Yes / No / Partly.
    2. Inside your budget range? Including the full cost of ownership, not just the sticker.
    3. Honours your top priorities? The ones you refused to compromise on.

    Options that fail a column drop out. You should be left with two or three — then the decision gets easy.

    Rule of thumb: if a spec doesn’t change which option you’d choose, it isn’t decision information. You don’t have to ignore it — you just have to table it.

    Don’t compare across categories

    Frame it correctly. “Which laptop?” and “Which phone?” are different questions with different criteria. Compare within one category and against your requirements, not between products that were never alternatives.

    When the stats are equal

    When two options genuinely match on everything you listed, stop comparing specs and start comparing the intangibles you can actually test: how it feels to use, how easy it is to set up, how good the support and returns are. Those are real differences that no spec sheet captures — and they’re usually the ones you’ll remember.
